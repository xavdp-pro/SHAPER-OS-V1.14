# Adopting an Outside Concept: Keep the Substance, Drop the Source

> **Status**: founding doctrine, stated by the operator on 17 September 2026. It governs
> how a concept from outside this work — a management method, a philosophy, a body of
> practice, a competitor's idea — enters this system.

## The law, in one line

**Keep what the concept does; state it plainly in our own words; never carry its
allegiance.** A concept enters here as a working tool, judged by what it produces, and
never as an argument from its origin.

## The four conditions

1. **Substance, not vocabulary.** Take the mechanism — what it makes visible, what it
   orders, what it refuses — and leave the terminology of its world behind. If the
   mechanism cannot be stated without its native jargon, it has not been understood yet.
2. **No reference to a philosophy, a religion, or a school**, in prose, in a link or in
   a footnote. This repository is read by clients, partners and agents who did not
   choose our reading list, and an allegiance printed beside a rule changes what the
   rule means to them. Where lineage genuinely matters for verification, name it in a
   dated design note outside the canon, never in a rule or a public document.
3. **Plain formulation.** The concept is rewritten so that a competent stranger can use
   it without any background: what it is for, what it asks, what it refuses, and what
   it costs. A concept that needs an initiation is not adopted, it is imported.
4. **Judged by what it does here.** It earns its place by producing a mechanism — a rule,
   a checklist, a refusal, a test, a stop condition. If it produces only a feeling of
   clarity, it stays a reading note. The same test the kernel corpus already applies to
   itself (`THE-KERNEL-IS-CONSULTED-NOT-CARRIED.md`).

## Why this is a rule and not taste

The operator reads widely — management writing, cognitive frameworks, practice
literature — and several mechanisms of this system came from outside it. Two failures
follow from adopting a concept badly, and both have been paid for:

* **The allegiance failure.** A document that cites its source's world hands the reader
  a second question — *do I accept that world?* — before the first one, *does this
  mechanism work?* A planning scale is useful whoever wrote it; printed with its
  origin, it stops being a tool and becomes a position.
* **The imported-jargon failure.** A concept carried with its original words spreads a
  second vocabulary through the system, and Rule 37 exists precisely because two words
  for one thing produce two models of it. Anything adopted here is spelled in the
  lexicon's terms or given a lexicon entry through Rule 37's amendment path.

## What it does not license

* **It is not an excuse to hide a debt.** If a mechanism is someone else's invention and
  the question of credit is real — a licence, a patent, a named method a client might
  recognise — the answer is a legal and commercial one, not a stylistic one. Ask the
  operator before publishing.
* **It is not a reason to soften the concept.** Stripping the origin never means
  stripping the rigour: an adopted mechanism keeps its refusals. A method that only
  works with the parts nobody enjoys removed was not adopted, it was diluted.
* **It does not apply to sources of fact.** A measurement, a vendor document, a standard
  or a specification is cited exactly, with its reference, because there the source *is*
  the evidence. This page is about concepts and methods, never about facts.

## The shape of an adoption

In the document where the mechanism now lives:

* what it is for, in one sentence;
* what it asks of us, as steps or conditions we can check;
* what it refuses, and what that refusal prevents here;
* the date it entered, and the mechanism it produced.

Nothing else. The reader gets a tool; the origin stays in the operator's library.
