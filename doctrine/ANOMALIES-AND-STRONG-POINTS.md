# Anomalies and Strong Points: Reading a Situation Before Deciding

> **Status**: founding doctrine, carried here on 17 September 2026 by operator decision.
> It is an adoption under [`ADOPTING-AN-OUTSIDE-CONCEPT.md`](./ADOPTING-AN-OUTSIDE-CONCEPT.md):
> the mechanism is kept, the vocabulary is ordinary, and nothing here asks a reader to
> know where it came from.

## What this is for

Before a situation can be handled it has to be read, and reading it means noticing two
kinds of signal in what we observe:

* an **anomaly** — an observation that does not hold together. It says *look here*, and
  nothing more: it is not yet a cause, and never a verdict on a person.
* a **strong point** — an observation that does hold together. It says *this part is
  sound*, and it matters as much: it tells us what not to break while repairing the rest,
  and it is where an unusual success can be found and kept.

A review that only collects anomalies produces a list of complaints; a review that only
collects strong points produces comfort. The pair is what makes an evaluation honest.

## The words we use, and the words we do not

Plain words, so nobody has to learn a dialect to run an evaluation
(Rule 37 closes the lexicon; this page adds no noun to the tree):

| We say | We mean |
| --- | --- |
| the scene we aim for | the state an area ought to reach, stated so it can be recognised when reached |
| the departure | the gap between what is and the scene we aim for |
| the major departure | the one gap worth handling now, out of all of them |
| the reason | what, once understood, opens the way to handling the major departure |
| the handling | the programme of targets that closes the gap |
| a measure | a number compared to its own earlier value, for the same thing |
| what the activity delivers | the finished thing others can use or exchange for |
| an anomaly | an observation that does not hold together (below) |
| a strong point | an observation that does hold together (below) |

## The twelve shapes an anomaly takes

They are shapes, not accusations, and they are worth knowing by heart because naming the
shape is most of the work:

1. **A fact is missing** — something needed to understand the situation was never stated.
2. **The order is wrong** — events are reported in a sequence they cannot have happened in.
3. **Time is missing** — an event has no date, so it cannot be placed or compared.
4. **A statement is untrue** — plainly contradicted by what can be observed.
5. **Importance is misjudged** — a detail treated as decisive, or a decisive thing treated as a detail.
6. **The target is wrong** — the right effort aimed at the wrong problem.
7. **The source is wrong** — a fact credited to something or someone that did not produce it.
8. **Two facts contradict each other** — both are stated, both cannot hold.
9. **Time is added** — an event placed in a period it could not occupy, or a duration invented.
10. **Something that does not apply is brought in** — true elsewhere, irrelevant here.
11. **A fact from another subject is counted here** — real, but it belongs to a different area.
12. **Two different things are treated as the same** — or two identical things treated as different.

One anomaly is a signal. Several of the same shape, in the same place, are usually the
trail to the reason.

## What a strong point looks like

The mirror image, and the list is deliberately short: facts that are verified; events in
the order they happened; times recorded; importance judged right; the target matching the
problem; a source that is actually the origin; facts that agree; data that applies here.

Two obligations follow, and they are the mechanism this page exists to produce:

* **Do not break a strong point while handling a departure.** A handling that fixes the
  gap and destroys what already worked is a new departure, not a repair.
* **An unusual success is recorded like a defect is.** Rule 29 turns every resolved
  defect into a test, so that a failure raises the floor permanently. The same courtesy
  is owed to an outcome that went unusually well: what made it possible is written where
  it survives — the brick's `INTENT.md` (Rule 35), and a test that keeps it — instead of
  being enjoyed once and forgotten. A system that learns only from its failures learns
  half of what happens to it.

## How an evaluation runs here

1. Describe the existing scene honestly, with its dates and its measures.
2. State the scene we aim for, in terms that can be recognised when reached.
3. Collect the anomalies and the strong points, by shape, without interpreting yet.
4. Name the major departure: the one gap whose handling changes the others.
5. Find the reason — the thing that, once understood, makes the handling obvious.
6. Write the handling as a programme of targets, and say which measure will show it worked.
7. Record what the handling must not break.

Steps 1 to 3 are observation, step 4 and 5 are interpretation, and the difference is
never blurred: that separation is the same one the kernel corpus asks for, and the same
one Rule 20 asks of a verification.

## Where this already applies

* The ideal-scene pages of a project (`podmesh/docs/IDEAL-SCENE.md`,
  `univ-vox-core/docs/IDEAL-SCENE.md`) are the scene we aim for, and their programme is
  the handling. They use this vocabulary.
